4 of 7

Some sensible-sounding practices made things worse

  • Public performance rankings lowered productivity in a sweater factory. Private rankings had no average effect.
  • Surprise bonuses lowered output in short data-entry jobs.
  • Adding explicit “potential” ratings to promotion decisions led to weaker promoted employees.

5 of 7

How a practice is run decides the result

  • Mentoring raised sales when it was mandatory. A voluntary version did not, because the people who needed it most did not sign up.
  • An AI rollout raised sales when it was fitted to how salespeople work, and lowered them when it was not.
  • Letting store managers hire raised average sales, but some stores lost.

Method and limits

How to read this

  1. Counts are not effects. Each study counts once, whatever its size or quality. There is no pooled “HR effect”, because the studies measure different things.
  2. The result codes are a first draft. An AI assistant assigned them from each study's reported finding, mostly taken from abstracts, and no person has reviewed them yet. Every study note shows the finding the code rests on, and the reasoning where the call was not obvious.
  3. Most checks were light.
  4. Published results lean positive. Studies that find nothing are less likely to be written up and published.
  5. This is not the whole literature. It covers 49 selected journals, screened by a single reviewer. It is a scoping review, not a systematic one.

How the studies were found

We pulled 9,600 metadata records from Crossref for 49 selected journals in industrial-organizational psychology, HR, management and economics, published from 1 January 2025. After removing non-research items, 9,229 remained. Title filters and targeted searches for causal designs narrowed these to 107 candidates, each assessed individually; 68 were kept.

The first online publication date decides whether a study falls in the window. Where only an issue date was available, the study note says so. One further candidate is listed separately because its original publication date could not be confirmed.

We did not use licensed databases such as PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science or EconLit.

What the outcome columns mean
Business output
Measured productivity, sales, innovation, quality, safety or time use. Broader than profit: only a few studies measure profit directly.
Turnover, absence, hiring
Retention, absence, recruitment, labor supply and workforce mix. Useful, but not by themselves proof of higher productivity or profit.
Rated or self-reported performance
Supervisor ratings, self-reports, creativity and learning measures.
Related settings
AI tools at work, public-sector staff, entrepreneur training and short lab or game tasks. Relevant, but further from everyday HR in private firms.
How the result codes were assigned

Each study gets one code for the direction of its main business outcome, using the study's own reported finding:

Improved
At least one tested version improved the outcome, and none worsened it.
Mixed or depends
Both gains and losses were reported: across versions, groups of people, settings, or between two business outcomes.
Worsened
At least one tested version worsened the outcome, and none improved it.
No detectable effect
No tested version changed the outcome detectably. This does not prove the effect is zero.
Unclear
The source we checked does not report a direction.

The codes were assigned by an AI assistant and have not yet been reviewed by a person. The full rules and every judgment call are in the codebook.

What each design can and cannot show

Randomized experiments show the effect of assigning the practice in that setting. They do not show that the effect carries over to other organizations.

Natural and quasi-experiments (difference-in-differences, thresholds, staggered rollouts) need the comparison group to have followed the same trend as the treated group would have without the practice.

Experiments whose randomization we could not confirm are labelled as such. We did not assume random assignment from the word “experiment”.

Observational studies with adjustment (fixed effects, matching, instruments, selection models) depend on assumptions that the data cannot fully test.

Journals covered

Chosen for prominence and relevance; no impact-factor cut-off. A journal with no study listed may still have published relevant work.

Excluded and borderline studies

Near misses, date exclusions and candidates that need a closer look at their methods. “Methods check needed” does not mean the study lacks a causal design.

Known gaps and how this will be kept current

Not yet verified: DOIs and online dates have not been checked against Crossref since the original collection; the automated check below will do this. Pre-registration has not been checked in trial registries: the 3 studies that state it in their design are marked, and the rest are marked “not checked”, not “no”.

Keeping it current: a scheduled GitHub workflow queries Crossref each month for new articles in the 49 journals and confirms that each listed DOI still resolves to the recorded title and date. It opens an issue listing new candidates for manual review. Nothing is added automatically.

What would make it more complete: human review of the result codes, a second independent coder, full-text checks of methods for every study, licensed database searches, citation chasing and a formal risk-of-bias assessment.
